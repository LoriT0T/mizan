# Domain-Grounding Research: Islamic Finance Contracts & Sharia Governance for a Kuwaiti Sharia-Review Copilot

> **Provenance note.** This is the domain-grounding research report referenced as Input #2 in the Mizan Stage 1a birth instructions. It was produced by a deep research pass over public authoritative sources (AAOIFI public materials, CBK publications, IFSB standards, OIC Fiqh Academy resolutions, regulator transpositions, and established Islamic-finance literature). It maintains an established-vs-provisional discipline throughout; preserve those labels when encoding from it. The full AAOIFI Sharia Standards text is copyrighted — this report references structure, standard numbers, and widely-cited public principles only, and the registry built from it must do the same.

## TL;DR
- **Murabaha is the cleanest first anchor**: its compliance rules are publicly documented, largely binary (ownership/possession, cost+markup disclosure, permissible existing asset), it dominates Kuwaiti Islamic banking, and it depends least on contested scholarly judgment — Tawarruq should be deferred because organized tawarruq is genuinely disputed (the OIC International Islamic Fiqh Academy ruled it impermissible in Resolution 179 (19/5), Sharjah, 26–30 April 2009; AAOIFI permits a constrained form).
- **The tool must anchor on CBK instructions and Higher Committee guidance FIRST, with AAOIFI as a second layer**: Kuwait uses AAOIFI standards voluntarily/by reference (unlike Bahrain, Qatar, Oman and the UAE, where they are mandatory — the UAE made them compulsory effective 1 September 2018), so the rule hierarchy is CBK Circular 2/IBS/369/2016 + Higher Committee of Shari'ah Supervision → each bank's own SSB fatwas → AAOIFI as persuasive reference.
- **The tool is a copilot, never a mufti**: it can retrieve standards, draft cited review memos, flag red flags, and produce bilingual documentation, but the Sharia *ruling* (fatwa) and sign-off must remain with the qualified human SSB. The full AAOIFI Sharia Standards text is copyrighted and sold by AAOIFI, so the tool may reference structure, topic coverage and widely-cited principles but must not reproduce proprietary text.

## Key Findings

1. **Three foundational prohibitions** drive nearly all contract-compliance checks: *riba* (interest/usury — two types: riba al-nasiah on deferred debt, riba al-fadl on unequal same-genus exchange), *gharar* (excessive uncertainty — prohibited when "fahish/excessive," tolerated when "yasir/minor"), and *maysir* (gambling/pure speculation). These are reinforced by structural requirements: asset-backing, real ownership and possession (qabd) before resale, the bar on selling what one does not own, and the risk-reward maxims *al-ghunm bil-ghurm* and *al-kharaj bil-daman* (entitlement to profit requires bearing liability/ownership risk).

2. **The three target contracts sit on a spectrum of compliance clarity**: Murabaha (cost-plus sale) and Ijara (lease) are asset-based and have relatively clear, well-documented conditions; Tawarruq (commodity monetisation for cash) is structurally suspect because it produces pure cash and is the locus of the industry's sharpest live disagreement (organized vs. classical tawarruq).

3. **Kuwait's Sharia-governance regime is now mature and auditable**: CBK Circular 2/IBS/369/2016 (effective 1 January 2018) sets the governance framework; external Sharia audit became mandatory from 1 January 2020; the Higher Committee of Shari'ah Supervision was established in 2020; and the Certified Shari'a Auditor credential is now a mandatory professional qualification for Sharia auditors at CBK- and CMA-regulated entities.

4. **A credible Sharia-review deliverable has a stable, documentable skeleton** — product/contract description → structure & mechanics → Shariah issues → standards/evidence cited → opinion → conditions/caveats → human sign-off — and non-compliance findings are documented as incident → clause/rule breached → severity → remediation (rectification / income purification) → status, escalated through a defined chain ending at the SSB and Board Audit Committee.

## Details

### PART 1 — Foundational Concepts

**Riba (interest/usury).** Riba literally means "excess" or "increase" and refers to any guaranteed, predetermined increase on a loan or debt. It is the most absolute of the prohibitions, grounded directly in the Qur'an (notably Surah Al-Baqarah 2:275–279, "Allah has permitted trade and forbidden riba"). Classical jurisprudence distinguishes two types:
- **Riba al-nasiah** (riba of delay/credit): the increase charged for deferment of payment on a loan or debt — i.e., conventional interest. This is the principal and most clear-cut form. A farmer borrowing 10,000 and repaying 11,000 purely for the passage of time incurs riba al-nasiah.
- **Riba al-fadl** (riba of excess): an unequal *simultaneous* exchange of the same genus of commodity (gold for gold, wheat for wheat, silver for silver) in differing quantities. It derives from hadith and extends to currency exchange of the same kind. Its prohibition is about equity in barter/exchange; a delayed (rather than simultaneous) exchange of such items falls under riba al-nasiah.

*Why prohibited:* riba is seen as exploitative and unjust — it lets the lender profit without sharing risk or productive effort, concentrating wealth and creating instability. *How it manifests in contracts:* the central compliance risk for the tool is detecting transactions that *look like* sales/leases but are in economic substance riba — e.g., a "Murabaha" where the bank never genuinely owns the asset (so its markup is interest on money, not profit on a sale), or late-payment penalties that accrue to the bank's income (which replicate compounding interest).

**Gharar (excessive uncertainty/ambiguity).** Gharar denotes uncertainty, ambiguity, hazard or deception in the essential elements of a contract (subject matter, price, delivery, existence). Unlike riba, no Qur'anic verse prohibits gharar by name; it derives from hadith and the broader prohibition of injustice. Jurists grade it:
- **Gharar fahish (excessive)** — vitiates the contract. Examples: selling fish not yet caught, selling the contents of a sealed box, making a contract conditional on an unknown future event, or combining interdependent contracts that create unresolvable ambiguity.
- **Gharar yasir (minor/trivial)** — tolerated, because removing all uncertainty is impossible and would "narrow the scope of transactions" (Al-Shatibi). Classical tolerated examples: selling a lined coat though the lining is unseen, selling a house though its foundations are unseen, renting for "a month" that may be 30 or 31 days.
- **Gharar mutawassit (moderate)** — assessed case-by-case in context.

Tolerance also applies where there is genuine public need (e.g., salam and istisna' are permitted despite selling things not yet existing) or where gharar is incidental to a charitable (tabarru') contract. *Compliance relevance:* the tool should flag undefined price/markup, undefined lease term or rental, undescribed/unidentified subject matter, and contingent-on-unknown-event clauses — but must recognise that minor uncertainty does not void a contract, which is a matter of scholarly judgment.

**Maysir (gambling/speculation).** Maysir is the acquisition of wealth by pure chance without productive effort or real economic exchange, prohibited by direct Qur'anic revelation (Al-Baqarah 2:219; Al-Maidah 5:90–91). It is distinct from gharar: maysir is wagering-based zero-sum gain/loss; gharar is contractual uncertainty. Sharia does *not* prohibit ordinary commercial risk-taking — that is intrinsic to legitimate trade. Because of maysir (combined with gharar), conventional derivatives (options, futures, swaps used purely for price speculation) and conventional insurance are generally impermissible; Takaful (mutual/cooperative insurance with tabarru' donations) is the Islamic alternative.

**Other core prohibitions and structural requirements.** Beyond the three, the tool's rule-set must encode: prohibition of *haram* subject matter (alcohol, pork, gambling, conventional financial services); the requirement of a valid *aqd* (offer/acceptance, capacity, lawful object); and — critically for asset-based finance — the doctrines that legitimise profit:
- **Possession before resale (qabd):** "Whoever buys food, let him not sell it until he has taken possession of it" (Bukhari/Muslim) — generalised by jurists to all goods. A bank cannot sell what it does not own and possess (actual or constructive).
- **al-ghunm bil-ghurm** ("gain accompanies liability") and **al-kharaj bil-daman** ("entitlement to revenue is matched by liability for loss," from a hadith narrated by al-Tirmidhi, Abu Dawud, Ibn Majah and Ahmad): to be entitled to a return, the owner of an asset/capital must bear the corresponding ownership and market risk. This is the doctrinal reason an Islamic lessor must keep ownership risk, and why a financier cannot earn a fixed return without risk.

**Sharia governance — authority structure.** A **Sharia Supervisory Board (SSB)** is a panel of qualified Islamic-law scholars (typically a minimum of three) that reviews and approves an institution's contracts, products and transactions, and issues **fatwas** (Sharia legal opinions/rulings). A fatwa is the "atomic" unit of Sharia — a reasoned legal opinion issued by a qualified jurist. **AAOIFI** (Accounting and Auditing Organization for Islamic Financial Institutions, founded 1991, based in Bahrain) is the leading international standard-setter; it issues Sharia Standards, accounting standards (FAS), governance standards (GSIFI/GS) and ethics codes, aiming to harmonise the fatwas of different SSBs. **IFSB** (Islamic Financial Services Board, Kuala Lumpur, operational 2003) issues prudential and governance guiding principles (notably IFSB-10 on Sharia governance) for regulators.

The decisive design principle: **a Sharia ruling requires a qualified human scholar; the supporting research, drafting, documentation and evidence-gathering can be tool-assisted.** IFSB-10 (Guiding Principles on Shari'ah Governance Systems, December 2009) itself frames the rendering of a Sharia opinion as "a matter of the professional judgement of members of a Sharī`ah board." The tool therefore operates entirely in the research/drafting/documentation layer and must structurally defer the *ruling* to the SSB.

### PART 2 — The Three Contract Types Compared

#### Murabaha (cost-plus-profit sale financing)

**Structure & mechanics (Murabaha to the Purchase Orderer, the banking form):**
1. Customer identifies an asset (e.g., a car, real estate, commodities) and requests the bank to finance it, typically promising to purchase it.
2. The bank purchases the asset from the supplier, taking genuine ownership and (actual or constructive) possession, thereby bearing ownership risk.
3. The bank sells the asset to the customer at a disclosed price = cost + agreed profit markup, usually on deferred/instalment terms (bai' muajjal).
4. Ownership transfers to the customer at the sale; the customer owes the deferred sale price as a debt. The bank's profit is the markup on a real sale, not interest on money.

**Mandatory Sharia conditions:** (a) the asset must exist and be permissible (halal); (b) the bank must genuinely own and possess it before reselling — "even for a very short time, with all risks and rewards incidental to ownership"; (c) full disclosure of cost and markup (this is what distinguishes Murabaha from musawamah, where cost need not be disclosed); (d) the price, markup and instalment schedule must be determined and fixed at contract; (e) the asset must not already belong to the customer (which would make it bai' al-inah).

**Common ways real contracts FAIL (red flags the tool should detect):**
- **No genuine ownership/possession by the bank** — the bank merely issues a cheque or pays the supplier on the customer's behalf while the customer acts as buying agent, so the bank never bears asset risk. This is the single most common defect and converts the transaction into a disguised interest loan. Kuwaiti courts reflect this: per Chambers' Islamic Finance 2025 (Kuwait), the judiciary "will not endorse artificial Murabaha arrangements" and requires proof "that the bank had, in fact, acquired the asset prior to its resale to the customer."
- **Sale executed before the bank takes title/possession** (bai' qabl al-qabd) — selling before possession is void.
- **The "asset" is the customer's own property** — collapses into bai' al-inah (sale-and-buyback), widely prohibited in the GCC.
- **Markup re-priced or increased on late payment** — replicates interest; penalties, if any, must go to charity, not bank income.
- **Murabaha used for pure cash needs** (salaries, utility bills, general liquidity) with no real underlying asset purchase.
- **Binding bilateral promise treated as the sale itself**, removing the bank's ownership-risk window.

**Scholarly disagreement:** The chief contested point is the **binding nature of the promise (wa'd)** and **Murabaha to the Purchase Orderer (MPO)** itself. AAOIFI's Murabaha standard holds the preliminary arrangement must *not* include a mutually binding promise between the two parties (a bilateral binding promise equals a prohibited contract with both counter-values deferred); a *unilateral* promise may be made binding. The OIC Fiqh Academy holds a promise is morally binding, and legally binding if conditional and the promisee incurred expense. A minority/Maliki-influenced view (and some banks) treat the promise as integral and binding. Some early scholars prohibited MPO where it includes a promise of profit. The tool must treat promise-bindingness as a configurable, SSB-determined parameter, not adjudicate it.

**AAOIFI structure:** Murabaha is **Sharia Standard No. 8, "Murabaha" (formerly "Murabaha to the Purchase Orderer")**. Publicly documented topic coverage includes: procedures preceding the contract (incl. the promise and its bindingness), the acquisition and possession requirement, guarantees/security, conditions of the contract and the price, and default/late-payment treatment. *The full clause text is copyrighted and sold by AAOIFI — only structure and widely-cited principles may be used.*

#### Ijara (leasing)

**Structure & mechanics:** Ijara transfers the *usufruct* (right to use) of an asset for a defined term against defined rent, while the lessor retains ownership. In the banking form the institution buys the asset and leases it to the customer. Two main variants:
- **Operating Ijara** — asset returns to the lessor at the end; lessor bears ownership costs.
- **Ijara Muntahia Bittamleek / Ijara wa Iqtina** (lease ending in ownership transfer) — combines a lease with a *separate* promise to sell or gift the asset to the lessee at term end. Widely used for home and vehicle finance.

**Mandatory Sharia conditions:** (a) the asset must be non-consumable and usufruct-yielding (a car, house, machine — not money, fuel or food consumed in use); (b) the asset must be precisely identified; (c) rent and lease term must be defined at contract (to avoid gharar); (d) the lessor must own the asset and bear **ownership risk** for the whole term — major maintenance, structural damage not caused by lessee, insurance (takaful), and risk of destruction; (e) the lessee bears only use-related costs and damage from misuse/negligence; (f) rent is due only while the asset is usable; (g) in lease-to-own, the ownership-transfer promise must remain legally **separate** from the lease.

**Common ways real contracts FAIL (red flags):**
- **Ownership risk shifted to the lessee** — making the lessee bear major maintenance, total-loss risk, and insurance — which turns the lease into a disguised interest-bearing finance lease (the most common defect).
- **Sale and lease fused** in Ijara Muntahia Bittamleek so the lessor retains ownership "in name only," collapsing the structure into a credit sale or impermissible hybrid.
- **Rent charged before delivery** of the usable asset.
- **Late-payment penalties retained as lessor income** rather than donated to charity.
- **Automatic ownership transfer baked into the lease** (rather than a separate promise), or rent continuing after the asset is destroyed.
- **Leasing an impermissible-use asset** (e.g., premises for a prohibited business).

**Scholarly disagreement:** Less contested than Tawarruq, but live debates exist over: the mechanics of the ownership-transfer promise in Ijara Muntahia Bittamleek; whether rentals may be benchmarked to a conventional rate index; the treatment of insurance/takaful cost recovery; and forward lease (Ijara mawsufah fi dhimmah). Jurisdictional practice differs (e.g., the JRI Resources v. Kuwait Finance House (Malaysia) litigation turned on ijara terms and SAC rulings).

**AAOIFI structure:** Ijara is **Sharia Standard No. 9, "Ijarah and Ijarah Muntahia Bittamleek."** Publicly documented coverage includes the promise to lease, acquisition of the asset, the lease contract and rentals, the lessor's and lessee's respective liabilities, and the modalities of ownership transfer. *Full text copyrighted.*

#### Tawarruq (commodity-based liquidity/cash financing)

**Structure & mechanics (organized/banking tawarruq):**
1. Customer needs cash. The bank sells the customer a commodity (commonly metals via the London Metal Exchange) on deferred payment at cost + markup.
2. The customer (often through the bank as agent) immediately sells the same commodity to a third party for spot cash.
3. The customer receives cash now and owes the deferred (higher) price to the bank over time.

The commodity is incidental; the economic result is cash now for more cash later. **Classical tawarruq** (the customer independently finds a third-party buyer, no pre-arrangement, no buy-back to the original seller) is widely accepted. **Organized tawarruq** (the bank arranges the whole chain, often as agent) is the contested form.

**Mandatory Sharia conditions (for the permitted form):** (a) real commodities that genuinely exist and can be possessed (not gold/silver/currency, which trigger riba al-fadl); (b) the commodity must be sold to a party *other than* the original seller — "the commodity should not return back to the seller by virtue of prior agreement" (this avoids bai' al-inah); (c) genuine sequential sales with real possession/risk transfer; (d) the financier should not act as the customer's selling agent where the customer bought from the bank — the customer should sell himself or via his own agent.

**Common ways real contracts FAIL (red flags):**
- **The bank acts as agent for both legs**, pre-arranging the onward sale — the OIC Fiqh Academy treats this simultaneous, pre-arranged chain as a deception masking riba.
- **No real commodity / no real possession** — "billions of dollars of commodity-based tawarruq" without matching commodity trade; certificates shuffled with no intention to possess.
- **Buy-back to the original seller** (collapses into bai' al-inah).
- **Commodity is irrelevant, valueless, defective, or insufficient in existence** to cover the transaction volume.
- **Interconditionality** — the two sales contractually contingent on each other.

**Scholarly disagreement (the most important "defer to human" zone):** This is the sharpest live disagreement in the industry. The **OIC International Islamic Fiqh Academy, Resolution 179 (19/5)**, adopted at its 19th session in Sharjah, UAE, 26–30 April 2009 (1–5 Jumādā al-Ūlā 1430H), ruled **both organized and reverse tawarruq impermissible**: "It is not permissible to execute both tawarruq (organised and reversed) because simultaneous transactions occurs between the financier and the mustawriq… in exchange for a financial obligation. This is considered a deception… Hence, the transaction is considered as containing the element of riba." **AAOIFI Sharia Standard No. 30 ("Monetization/Tawarruq")** permits tawarruq subject to strict conditions (notably Article 4/5: sale to a third party, no return to the original seller; and 4/7–4/10 restricting the bank's agency role). **Malaysia (Bank Negara's Shariah Advisory Council)** broadly permits organized tawarruq and even allows agency (wakalah) arrangements that AAOIFI restricts — a clear **GCC vs. Malaysia divergence**. Indonesia's DSN-MUI (Fatwa 82/2011) treats tawarruq munazzam as problematic. The tool must **never adjudicate** organized tawarruq — it must surface the divergent positions and route to the SSB.

**AAOIFI structure:** Tawarruq is **Sharia Standard No. 30, "Monetization (Tawarruq)."** Related standards: No. 20 ("Sale of Commodities in Organized Markets") and No. 18 ("Possession/Qabd"). *Full text copyrighted.*

#### Comparison & recommendation

| Dimension | Murabaha (SS 8) | Ijara (SS 9) | Tawarruq (SS 30) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Asset basis | Real existing asset sold | Usufruct of owned asset | Commodity used only to generate cash |
| Compliance clarity | High — largely binary checks | Medium-high | Low — substance-over-form, contested |
| Dependence on contested judgment | Low (except wa'd bindingness) | Medium | Very high (organized tawarruq) |
| Public documentation of rules | Strong | Strong | Strong but contested |
| Prevalence in Kuwait | Very high (most common tool) | High (home/vehicle/equipment) | Common for liquidity |

**Recommendation: anchor the first demonstrable build on Murabaha.** Its conditions are the most binary and checkable (does the bank take ownership/possession before resale? is cost+markup disclosed? does the asset exist and is it permissible? is the asset already the customer's? are late penalties routed to income?), its rules are the best publicly documented, it depends least on contested scholarly judgment, and it is the most common financing tool in Kuwaiti Islamic banking — which is itself substantial: per Fitch Ratings (cited late 2025), the share of Islamic banking assets in the Kuwaiti banking sector has exceeded 49%, and Chambers' Islamic Finance 2025 (Kuwait) describes Murabaha as widely used across retail and corporate finance. Ijara is a strong second (clear ownership-risk tests but more variants). **Tawarruq should be explicitly deferred** as a first anchor precisely because its core question (organized tawarruq) is unresolved between the OIC Fiqh Academy and AAOIFI — it is the worst candidate for a tool meant to demonstrate clear-cut compliance checking, though it is an excellent showcase for the tool's "defer to human on contested matters" behaviour.

### PART 3 — Kuwait-Specific Regulatory/Governance Layer

**CBK Circular 2/IBS/369/2016 ("Shariah Supervisory Governance in Kuwaiti Islamic Banks").** Issued 20 December 2016 to replace 2003 instructions; banks had to comply by 31 December 2017 with full effect from 1 January 2018. It is explicitly guided by IFSB's December 2009 Guiding Principles and AAOIFI's governance standards. Its structure: Definitions → General Requirements → Five Principles (Oversight/Accountability/Responsibility; Independence; Efficiency & Fitness; Confidentiality; Consistency) → Sharia Audit (internal and external pillars) → Annexes (organizational structure of Sharia audit; SSB performance-appraisal form). It defines the roles of the Board of Directors, executive management and the SSB, and establishes the internal and external Sharia audit functions. Management bears responsibility for Sharia compliance; the SSB inspects all contracts, agreements, policies and transactions and issues binding fatwas the bank must implement.

**SSB requirements:** minimum three scholars of recognised competence in transactions jurisprudence (fiqh al-muamalat); independent of the board, executive management, and any 5%+ shareholder; the bank must publish the SSB's fatwas and resolutions.

**Mandatory external Sharia audit since 1 January 2020.** A separate CBK circular (dated 6 January 2020) required external Sharia audit from 1 January 2020 and CBK prior approval of the appointed external Sharia auditing office. In practice the external auditor opines that the bank's contracts, operations and activities comply with Sharia per the SSB's fatwas — conducted per CBK's 2016 instructions, AAOIFI's External Sharia Audit Standard (No. 6), and ISAE 3000 (assurance engagements), as illustrated in Warba Bank's external Sharia audit report for FY2020.

**Higher Committee of Shari'ah Supervision (2020).** Established under Law No. 3 of 2020 (issued 10 March 2020); the CBK Board approved its formation on 1 September 2020 and, on 5 October 2020, the Governor appointed four Fiqh/Islamic-banking specialists for a renewable three-year term: Dr. Essa Z. E. Shaqra (Chairman), Dr. Khaled M. Y. Boodi, Mostafa Sayed H. A. Alzelzelah, and Dr. Motlaq J. M. Aljaser. It reports directly to the CBK Board and: advises CBK on Sharia conformity of CBK's own transactions and instructions to Islamic banks; proposes general Sharia guidelines for products/services; proposes rules governing Sharia supervision and internal/external Sharia audit; **pre-approves SSB candidates**; **issues binding decisions where a bank's SSB members disagree or fail to reach a definitive opinion**; and gives opinions to courts/arbitration centres. An important nuance: academic analysis observes its primary practical role to date is *dispute resolution between banks' SSBs* rather than centralised fatwa issuance — Kuwait remains "largely self-regulatory," historically anchored by Kuwait Finance House's SSB as a de facto reference.

**Certified Shari'a Auditor credential (now mandatory).** Run under the CBK "Kafa'a" initiative and delivered by the Kuwait Institute of Banking Studies (KIBS); CBK Governor Basel A. Al-Haroon announced the sixth round on 29 January 2025. The program runs six weeks / 120 training hours in three phases. The certificate is a **mandatory professional qualification** to practise as an internal or external Sharia auditor at CBK- and CMA-regulated Islamic financial institutions.

**How Kuwait treats AAOIFI — "by reference/influence," not full legal mandate.** AAOIFI itself lists Kuwait as a jurisdiction where its standards are "recommended as guidelines"/used voluntarily as the basis of internal guidelines by regulators and leading institutions — contrasted with Bahrain, Jordan, Oman, Qatar, Sudan, Syria, and (since 2018) the UAE, where AAOIFI Sharia standards are mandatory regulatory requirements. Per AAOIFI's official announcement, the Central Bank of the UAE, on recommendation of its Higher Sharia Authority, required all full-fledged Islamic banks, Islamic windows and finance companies to comply with AAOIFI standards "with effect from 1 September 2018." **Practical implication for the tool's rule hierarchy:** (1) CBK instructions and Higher Committee guidance are the primary, binding layer; (2) the individual bank's own SSB fatwas and resolutions are binding on that bank; (3) AAOIFI Sharia Standards are a persuasive, widely-followed reference layer that the tool should cite but never treat as automatically binding in Kuwait. Note also that CBK does not itself adjudicate Sharia conformity of financing — it mandates the governance function and relies on the SSB to "bless" compliance.

**CBK-mandated Sharia audit — documentation/evidence.** The audit (internal and external) examines whether all contracts, products and transactions comply with Sharia per SSB fatwas. Evidence/working papers in practice include: the bank's policies, memorandum and procedures; SSB minutes, resolutions and fatwas; samples of contracts/agreements both endorsed and not endorsed by the SSB; new-product approval procedures; and testing of each transaction type (per CBK's required "testing each type of transactions" and AAOIFI's risk-based approach). The external auditor must confirm that new/modified products went through proper SSB review and that management complied with the SSB's fatwas.

**Bilingual Arabic-English documentation.** Kuwaiti Islamic banks publish in both Arabic and English. The governing convention from AAOIFI is that where the Arabic and English texts differ, **the Arabic text prevails** — but this is document-type-dependent: for Sharia pronouncements, AAOIFI-aligned texts and onshore Kuwaiti/GCC legal instruments, Arabic governs; for English-law-governed cross-border sukuk/financing documents and DIFC/ADGM instruments, the English text is typically the governing language with Arabic for convenience. The tool should let the author flag the authoritative language per document, not hard-code one rule.

**Applicable IFSB guidance.** IFSB-10 (Guiding Principles on Sharia Governance Systems for IIFS, December 2009) is the key reference (and was an explicit input to CBK's 2016 framework); it requires four organs — a Sharia board, an internal Sharia compliance review/audit (ISRU), an external Sharia compliance review/audit, and an internal Sharia compliance unit (ISCU) — and recommends consensus-first SSB decision-making, falling back to simple majority. IFSB also recognises "no single model" of Sharia governance across jurisdictions. Newer relevant standards: IFSB-30 (Revised Corporate Governance, 2023) and IFSB-31 (Effective Supervision of Sharia Governance, 2025), which add explicit non-compliance escalation/reporting expectations. The Governor of the CBK has chaired the IFSB Council, signalling Kuwait's close alignment with IFSB.

### PART 4 — What a Credible "Sharia-Review Memo" Deliverable Actually Looks Like (Output Design)

**The canonical annual SSB report skeleton** (from CBK's instructions, which mirror AAOIFI GSIFI No. 1):
1. Title + addressee (the shareholders);
2. Scope of work — nature of examination performed, including assurance that documentation and procedures were inspected "through testing each type of transactions";
3. Statement that the SSB was provided all information/explanations needed for its opinion;
4. The opinion — whether contracts, documents and transactions comply with Sharia;
5. Exceptions — any breaches of Sharia or of the SSB's own fatwas must be stated.

**The de facto GCC "four confirmations" template** (e.g., Kuwait Finance House's Fatwa & Shari'a Supervisory Board report): (1) contracts/transactions during the year complied with Sharia and the SSB's resolutions; (2) profit distribution and loss-bearing on investment accounts complied; (3) income from non-compliant sources was cleansed/donated to charity; (4) Zakat was calculated correctly — followed by named member signatures. (Zakat confirmation varies by jurisdiction, so make it optional.)

**Product-level Sharia review/structuring memo — recommended section inventory** (synthesised from AAOIFI SS 29 on fatwa ethics, the AAOIFI Shari'ah Decision-Making process standard, and GCC practice):
- Product/transaction description
- Contract structure & mechanics (the underlying aqd — murabaha, ijara, wakala, etc., with the sequence of ownership transfers)
- Shariah issues identified
- Shariah basis/evidence (Qur'an/Sunnah, OIC Fiqh Academy resolutions, AAOIFI Sharia Standards cited by number, prior SSB fatwas)
- The opinion/ruling (permissible / impermissible / permissible-with-conditions)
- Conditions & caveats (shurut)
- Implementation/monitoring requirements
- Date + signatures of SSB members

**How non-compliance findings are presented.** Best practice (per an ISRA International Journal of Islamic Finance study and IFSB-31) documents each finding as a matrix row: event/incident description → the clause/contract at issue → the Sharia rule or fatwa breached → **severity** (the ISRA study proposes minor / moderate / major; note this is a best-practice academic scheme, not a binding AAOIFI scale, so make tiers configurable) → **risk type** (reputational / financial / regulatory) → person/owner responsible → **recommended remediation** → status. A real worked example from KFH's SSB report: a branch concluded a contract containing a late-payment penalty clause the SSB does not approve; the defect was identified and "it has been rectified"; in another, income from a non-compliant currency-trading activity was ordered "cleansed" and the cleansing "complied." **Remediation types**: rectification of the contract/process, purification of non-compliant income (channel to charity), divestment of non-compliant assets, or — where the defect is fundamental — voiding.

**Citations to standards and prior fatwas.** Findings and opinions should cite by specific reference: AAOIFI Sharia Standard number and clause (acknowledging the text is proprietary), OIC Fiqh Academy resolution number (e.g., Resolution 179), the bank's own prior SSB fatwa/resolution, and CBK/Higher Committee instructions. Academic Islamic-law citation convention (per the Harvard SHARIAsource style guide) is that jurists/fatwa bodies "conclude, determine, argue" — they do not "rule/hold/decide" as a court would — which the tool's language should respect.

**Working papers (internal/external Sharia audit).** Comprise: the Sharia audit charter (approved by the SSB and Board); the audit plan (objective, scope, sampling, personnel, duration); reference materials (SSB resolutions, fatwas, AAOIFI standards, internal Sharia checklists); sampled contracts and evidence (both SSB-endorsed and non-endorsed); test results by transaction type; findings with severity and rectification status; and follow-up on remediation. AAOIFI GSIFI No. 2 ("Shariah Review") structures the process as planning → execution → documenting conclusions → reporting. The current internal-audit standard is **AAOIFI GS 11 "Internal Shari'ah Audit"** (which supersedes the 1999 GSIFI No. 3 "Internal Shari'ah Review").

**Sign-off chain and placement of the human scholar.** The documented chain is: **internal Sharia auditor (preparer) → Head of Internal Sharia Audit → SSB/FSSB (resolves the Sharia matter; its resolution cannot be overridden by management or the audit committee) → Board Audit Committee → Board of Directors**, with the annual SSB report going to shareholders (and, in Kuwait, the framework reported to CBK). In the UAE the annual ISSC report must go to the Higher Sharia Authority for approval before shareholders — a useful comparator. The defining feature for the tool: **the human SSB's resolution is the authoritative act and cannot be substituted; the tool's output is an input to that human decision, never the decision.**

**Bilingual layout.** GCC documents are typically dual-column (Arabic right / English left) or stacked parallel, with an explicit governing-language clause. As above, the authoritative language is document-type-dependent (Arabic prevails for Sharia/onshore instruments; English often governs English-law sukuk/financing docs). The tool should generate true parallel bilingual output and require the author to designate the authoritative language per document.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Build the Murabaha rule-set first.** Encode the binary checks: (1) does the bank take genuine ownership + actual/constructive possession before resale? (2) is cost + markup disclosed? (3) does the asset exist and is it permissible? (4) is the asset already the customer's (bai' al-inah risk)? (5) are late-payment charges routed to charity rather than bank income? (6) is the wa'd unilateral vs. bilaterally binding (flag, don't adjudicate)? Anchor each rule to its source layer (CBK → SSB fatwa → AAOIFI SS 8). *Benchmark to advance:* the tool reliably flags the "no genuine possession" pattern and produces a memo a human SSB rates as a usable first draft.

**Stage 2 — Add Ijara.** Encode ownership-risk allocation checks (major maintenance/insurance/total-loss risk must stay with lessor), the separation of lease from ownership-transfer promise, rent-only-while-usable, and pre-delivery rent. *Benchmark:* correct detection of risk-shift-to-lessee defects.

**Stage 3 — Add Tawarruq only as a "deferral showcase."** Do not let the tool issue any determination on organized tawarruq; instead, have it detect the structural red flags (bank as dual agent, no real possession, buy-back to original seller, interconditionality) and present the OIC Fiqh Academy (Resolution 179, impermissible) vs. AAOIFI SS 30 (constrained permission) vs. Malaysia (broader permission) positions, then route to the SSB. This demonstrates the copilot's defer-to-human discipline.

**Cross-cutting design rules:**
- **Rule hierarchy:** CBK instructions + Higher Committee guidance (binding) → the bank's own SSB fatwas (binding on that bank) → AAOIFI standards (persuasive reference). Make the layer explicit in every citation.
- **Copyright boundary:** reference AAOIFI structure, standard numbers, topic coverage and widely-cited principles; never reproduce proprietary clause text. Surface this limitation in the UI and in generated memos.
- **Output format:** generate the product-memo skeleton (description → structure → issues → cited evidence → [opinion placeholder for SSB] → conditions → sign-off block) and a non-compliance matrix (incident → clause → rule/fatwa → configurable severity → risk type → owner → remediation → status).
- **Human-in-the-loop is structural, not cosmetic:** the "opinion/ruling" field is always empty until a named human SSB member completes it; the tool watermarks every draft as "research/drafting aid — not a fatwa."
- **Bilingual:** produce true parallel Arabic-English output with an author-set authoritative-language flag per document.
- **Defer-on-disagreement:** maintain an explicit register of contested matters (organized tawarruq, wa'd bindingness, rate-benchmarking, bai' al-inah) where the tool presents divergent authoritative positions and refuses to adjudicate.

**Thresholds that would change the approach:** if Kuwait's Higher Committee moves from dispute-resolution to issuing binding standardised contracts/fatwas (anticipated in the literature), elevate Higher Committee outputs to the top binding layer and reduce reliance on per-bank SSB divergence. If CBK were to mandate AAOIFI standards (as the UAE did effective 1 September 2018), promote AAOIFI from persuasive to binding in the hierarchy.

## Caveats

- **Established vs. provisional:** The foundational prohibitions (riba/gharar/maysir), the contract mechanics, AAOIFI standard numbers (SS 8 Murabaha, SS 9 Ijara, SS 30 Tawarruq), the OIC Fiqh Academy 2009 ruling (Resolution 179 (19/5)), and the CBK regime (Circular 2/IBS/369/2016; mandatory external audit from 1 Jan 2020; Higher Committee 2020; Certified Sharia Auditor) are well-established and well-sourced. The "minor/moderate/major" severity scheme and the precise product-memo section list are best-practice syntheses (academic + regulator-mirrored), not single binding texts.
- **Copyright/proprietary boundary:** The full AAOIFI Sharia Standards and Governance Standards texts are copyrighted and sold by AAOIFI; the clause-level content described here is drawn from public summaries, regulator transpositions (CBK, SBP, CBUAE) that mirror AAOIFI, and academic analysis — exact clause wording must be verified against purchased AAOIFI texts before any production use.
- **Scholarly disagreement is real and must be deferred, not resolved:** organized tawarruq (OIC Fiqh Academy vs. AAOIFI vs. Malaysia), the bindingness of the wa'd, bai' al-inah (prohibited in the GCC, practised in Malaysia), and rate-benchmarking are genuine live disputes. A compliance tool must surface positions and defer to the human SSB.
- **Religious dimension:** the tool assists qualified human scholars with research, drafting and documentation; it never issues fatwas or religious rulings. This is both a doctrinal requirement (a ruling needs a qualified human jurist) and a regulatory one (CBK vests the opinion in the SSB).
- **Single-source / verify-before-build items:** the exact current AAOIFI governance-standard numbering (GSIFI 1/2/3 vs. the 2018–2020 GS 19–22 / GS 11 restructuring) is in flux; the precise required headings of the current GS 21 (SSB report) and GS 11 (internal Sharia audit) should be verified against the purchased AAOIFI texts. The characterisation of the Higher Committee's role as primarily dispute-resolution is from academic analysis and should be reconfirmed against current CBK practice. The Fitch figure that Kuwaiti Islamic banking assets exceed 49% of the sector is a recent point estimate and will drift over time.
